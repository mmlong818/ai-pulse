ByteDance opens Seedance 2.5 to creators worldwide

ByteDance's Seedance 2.5 left closed enterprise beta on Friday, launching on Jimeng in China and Dreamina globally with 30-second single-pass generation.

Out of enterprise beta

ByteDance made Seedance 2.5 publicly available on 31 July, ending a closed enterprise beta that had run since the model was first shown in late June. The rollout came through the company's own creative apps: Jimeng in China and Dreamina internationally, which announced a global launch on X and positioned itself as the model's official platform.

The headline capability is length in one pass. Seedance 2.5 generates up to 30 seconds natively — double its predecessor — and supports extension to roughly three minutes of continuous material. It accepts as many as 50 reference inputs in a single prompt, and Dreamina highlights timing control described as accurate to within a second, letting creators specify when a beat, cut or action lands rather than re-rolling until the pacing works.

Aimed at production pipelines

The editing feature set is where ByteDance is pushing hardest against rivals. The model handles region-level changes — modifying part of a frame, removing or inserting elements, swapping characters — while holding character identity and visual continuity across shots. It also ingests inputs from professional workflows, including green-screen footage and untextured 3D blockouts, and ByteDance is shipping plugins for Maya and Blender aimed at game and animation teams.

Chinese coverage framed the release as a shift in intent: where Seedance 2.0 sold visual spectacle, 2.5 is built to slot into an existing pipeline and cut the number of iterations needed before a shot is usable in advertising, film and games.

Why it matters

The generative video field has been converging on a single bottleneck — controllability, not fidelity — and Seedance 2.5 attacks it with the levers studios already understand: reference stacks, region edits, second-level timing and DCC plugins. Arriving a day after MiniMax's H3 rolled out across Runway, Pika and Krea, it also confirms that the most aggressive competition in AIGC video is now between Chinese labs, with distribution through their own consumer apps rather than Western creative platforms. For working teams, the open question is whether 30 seconds of controllable footage is enough to displace conventional shot-by-shot assembly.
