Meta Terms Could Permit Training on Linked Business Sites

Meta’s upcoming commercial terms may let it crawl and use linked business websites for AI development, with no clear opt-out.

Meta is preparing commercial terms that could grant the company broad permission to crawl and use content from websites linked to business accounts, including for AI training. Ars Technica reported on October 16 that the revised terms are scheduled to take effect on October 30 and may apply to businesses using Meta’s advertising, pages, groups, messaging, or business-management services.

Consent through continued use

The proposed arrangement is significant because it appears to make continued use of Meta’s commercial tools the practical form of consent. Businesses that reject the terms may have to stop using Meta services rather than simply disable AI-related data collection. Legal experts told Nieman Lab that the language could be used to support Meta’s access to linked news and commercial sites, although enforceability remains uncertain.

The scope is broader than a conventional social-platform content license. It potentially reaches material hosted outside Meta’s services, including pages that a business links from its Facebook or Instagram presence. The exact implementation, crawler behavior, treatment of robots.txt, and boundaries around customer or user-submitted content remain important unanswered questions.

Meta has not thereby secured a final legal ruling that all such crawling and training is lawful. Contractual language can create a commercial position, but it does not automatically settle copyright, privacy, competition, or jurisdictional disputes. The policy could also provoke different reactions from publishers, small businesses, and regulators in different markets.

Why it matters

The proposal reflects a broader shift in AI data strategy: platforms with large business ecosystems can seek training rights through service contracts rather than negotiating individually with every publisher or site owner. That could lower Meta’s licensing costs and expand the volume of material available for model development.

For businesses, the immediate issue is not whether Meta wins a court case. It is whether access to an essential marketing channel becomes conditional on accepting a data-use clause that reaches beyond the platform itself. If the terms survive legal and regulatory scrutiny, they could become a model for other platforms. If they trigger coordinated resistance, they may instead clarify where contractual consent stops and compulsory access begins.
