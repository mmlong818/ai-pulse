OpenAI tests GPT-6 for laboratory protein design

OpenAI says GPT-6 models can design protein structures with general reasoning, while laboratory tests found binding signals in 35 of 322 tested minibinders and still relied on specialist models for sequence design and candidate selection.

What happened

OpenAI published research showing that its GPT-6 family can propose protein structures using general reasoning and coding tools, without directly calling a specialist protein-design model for the initial three-dimensional designs. The work was conducted with research partner Onava and includes computational evaluations and laboratory tests.

In the first laboratory experiment, OpenAI tested 322 minibinders designed for five targets. Thirty-five designs, or 10.9%, showed binding to their intended targets: 18 for IL-17A, eight for VEGF-A, five for PD-L1, three for IL-7Rα and one for TrkA. OpenAI also reports that GPT-6.2 designed new amino-acid sequences for existing minibinders and antibodies, with many redesigned proteins showing evidence of binding after specialist-model candidate selection.

What remains unproven

The results do not establish an end-to-end, zero-shot protein-design system. GPT-6.2 generated the three-dimensional minibinder designs, but SolubleMPNN was used to propose amino-acid sequences, while other specialist models helped select candidates predicted to fold correctly and bind at the intended site. OpenAI also reports a substantial gap in complete protein-structure prediction: its latest internal model performed better than GPT-6.2 on several evaluations, while complete structure prediction remained a weakness for the GPT-6 models.

Binding is only an early milestone. Researchers still need to test specificity, stability, biological activity, safety and therapeutic efficacy.

Why it matters

The important development is the widening role of a general-purpose model in molecular research. If natural-language instructions can increasingly guide candidate structure generation before specialist tools and wet-lab validation take over, drug-discovery workflows may become more programmable. For now, the evidence supports a capable research collaborator, not an autonomous medicinal-chemistry engine.

Uncle Cat take: Thirty-five binding signals out of 322 tested minibinders are meaningful early evidence, but specialist-model scaffolding and low overall hit rates keep this from being a self-sufficient biology breakthrough.
