UK CMA probes Microsoft over Copilot-driven price rises

Britain's regulator is examining whether Microsoft misled consumers when it bundled Copilot into Microsoft 365 and rolled subscribers onto costlier plans.

The UK Competition and Markets Authority opened a consumer-protection investigation into Microsoft on Wednesday, examining whether customers were given adequate information about their options when Copilot was bundled into Microsoft 365 Personal and Family subscriptions.

The conduct at issue

From January 2025, Microsoft granted existing consumer subscribers access to new features including Copilot at no additional charge for the remainder of their term. At renewal, those customers were moved automatically onto a new, more expensive plan carrying those features unless they took action to switch plans or cancel. For annual Personal and Family subscribers, the new plan cost £25 a year more than the equivalent "Classic" plan, which preserved the prior feature set at the prior price.

Microsoft did make Classic plans available, but only as a time-limited option. The CMA's stated concern is whether pre-renewal communications made that alternative clear enough for customers to exercise it, or whether the default path quietly routed them into paying more for a capability they had not chosen.

This is a distinct proceeding from the CMA's strategic market status investigation into Microsoft's business software ecosystem, opened on 14 May 2026 and covering Windows, Teams and enterprise Copilot licensing. The two run on separate tracks under separate powers — the new case sits under the authority's consumer-protection regime, not competition law.

Microsoft faces parallel exposure elsewhere: Australia's competition regulator has already sued the company over allegedly misleading Microsoft 365 pricing arising from the same bundling change.

Why it matters

Bundling AI features into an existing subscription and letting renewal do the repricing has been the fastest route to consumer AI revenue at scale, and Microsoft ran the largest such experiment. Two regulators on two continents now treating the same mechanic as a potential consumer-law problem sets a template that applies to every incumbent contemplating the same move — Google, Adobe, Apple and the rest.

The substantive question is not whether AI features may be charged for, but whether they can be added by default. If regulators land on requiring affirmative opt-in for AI-driven price increases, the economics of monetising AI through installed bases get materially worse, and vendors lose the mechanism that has made those upgrades look voluntary.
