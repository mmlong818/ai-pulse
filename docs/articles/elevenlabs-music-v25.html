ElevenLabs Releases Music v2.5 for Commercial Creation

ElevenLabs has released Music v2.5 with longer compositions, vocal control, genre transitions, and commercial-use terms through its creator and API products.

ElevenLabs has released Music v2.5, a new generation model for producing finished songs from an idea, sound, or loop. The company says the system supports long-form compositions, mid-track genre changes, rap, lyrics, and vocals, while improving melody, instrumentation, and arrangement depth.

From soundtrack tool to production system

Music v2.5 is available through ElevenMusic and ElevenCreative, with an API for programmatic generation. The product is aimed at advertising, branded content, video production, and other commercial workflows rather than only casual experimentation. ElevenLabs says users can generate tracks with reference material, inpainting, and longer-form control. Its free plan includes five downloads per day, with commercial use allowed when users provide attribution under the stated terms.

The release expands ElevenLabs beyond speech synthesis. Its existing strength in voice cloning and audio generation gives it a distribution path into music workflows where voice, dialogue, sound design, and soundtrack creation increasingly overlap. That convergence matters to studios and marketing teams that want to produce complete audiovisual packages without stitching together several specialist tools.

The unresolved constraint is rights

The commercial positioning is notable, but it does not settle the hardest questions around training data, style imitation, performer consent, and ownership of generated compositions. Product-level permission to use a downloaded track commercially is not the same as universal legal certainty across every market or use case.

Music v2.5 is therefore more important as a sign of product maturation than as proof that generative music has crossed a quality threshold. ElevenLabs is betting that controllable structure and a clearer business workflow will matter more to customers than novelty alone. Adoption will depend on whether creators trust the rights framework as much as they like the outputs.
