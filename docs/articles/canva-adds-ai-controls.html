Canva gives users a formal switch to disable AI features

Canva’s new controls let users turn generative features off and limit AI use, making consent a product setting rather than a policy promise.

A control layer for an AI-heavy design platform

Canva has introduced controls that let users switch generative-AI features on or off and limit how AI is used across the platform. The company announced the change on November 19, presenting it as a choice between fully assisted creation, selective AI use, and a more traditional manual workflow.

The move is notable because Canva is not retreating from AI. Its product roadmap has increasingly centered on conversational design, automated layout, image generation, brand intelligence, and agentic workflows. Instead, the company is separating the availability of those capabilities from the basic ability to use Canva as a design tool.

That distinction matters for schools, regulated organizations, brand teams, and creators who may want AI assistance in some projects but not others. It also gives administrators and individual users a clearer answer to a question that has often been buried in privacy settings: when is AI active, and how much control does the user have over it?

Canva’s announcement does not eliminate every unresolved issue. A feature toggle may control generation or personalization without fully explaining how existing content is stored, reviewed, or used for future model improvement. The practical value will depend on whether settings are visible, granular, and respected across collaborators, templates, and connected products.

Why it matters

The announcement turns AI consent into a visible product feature. As creative software becomes increasingly agentic, the ability to decline automation may become part of platform trust—and a competitive differentiator for buyers who need predictable human control.

Uncle Cat take

Canva’s important move is not the button itself; it is admitting that “AI everywhere” is a governance choice users should be able to reverse.
